    Case study · Own product · AI-first · Voice

    I'm building Callout, an MCP connector that lets Claude call you when you drop a habit

    Callout is at concept stage. This page is the plan: what I believe, how I will test it, and what AI product management means for a voice product. Nothing here is measured yet. I will add results as they come in.

    6 min readStatus: concept, build not startedLiving document
    Question
    Claude already sees your calendar. Can it also hold you to your habits by calling you when you skip one?
    Approach
    An MCP server lets Claude or any other agent assign a Callout to a habit. A rules layer decides whether a call may happen. An AI voice call then asks what got in the way and offers a new slot.
    Status
    Concept written. Five hypotheses, one eval set and one test design are planned. No results yet.
    Principle
    The rules decide whether to call. The model only holds the conversation.

    01 · The betA conversation creates more commitment than a notification

    Push reminders are easy to swipe away. A call asks for an answer. That is my core bet, and it is a hypothesis, not a fact.

    Two things make it testable now. Real-time voice models make a phone conversation cheap and natural enough to try. And AI agents already sit next to the user's calendar, so a habit does not need its own app to be known. Callout is therefore a capability for agents first. A companion app only enables the call: consent, phone number, rules and delivery.

    02 · HypothesesWhat has to be true

    Each hypothesis has a metric. Thresholds are set after a baseline week, so that they rest on measured values and not on hope.

    UntestedH1 · Calls beat pushes
    Users complete more planned sessions per week with a call than with a normal push reminder.Metric: share of sessions done, against the baseline week without calls
    UntestedH2 · Make-up slots stick
    When the call offers a replacement slot, more missed sessions are made up within 48 hours.Metric: share of missed sessions caught up within 48 hours
    UntestedH3 · Control builds trust
    Users accept calls if they set the limits: quiet hours, calls per week, highest escalation stage.Metrics: opt-out rate, "too intrusive" feedback, uninstalls
    UntestedH4 · Escalation learns
    Which stage works differs by person, weekday and time of day. Simple counts per user are enough to pick the stage.Metric: success rate per stage and time slot
    UntestedH5 · The unit cost works
    A call costs little enough that a monthly price for an active user can cover it.Metric: cost per call, cost per active user and month

    03 · ExperimentsHow I will test them

    Baseline week. Testers use the plan without any calls. This gives the starting values for H1 and H2.

    Call against push. 10 to 12 testers over two weeks, in two groups where possible. The sample is small. I will say so in the results and only talk about tendencies.

    Voice stack comparison. Two or three variants run against the same eval set: a pipeline of speech recognition, language model and speech output, and a real-time speech model. The choice rests on cost per call and latency, plus the eval score.

    Caller number. A German number needs a registered business. A foreign number is easier but is probably picked up less. I will measure the pick-up rate for what I can use.

    Web push reliability. Delivery depends on browser, battery settings and do-not-disturb. On the iPhone it only works once the web app sits on the home screen. I test on several devices in week two. If stage one fails, the phone call still works.

    04 · VoiceWhat a voice product asks of a product manager

    A chat answer can take two seconds. On a phone call it cannot. For the voice part I track three things from the first prototype:

    Time to first response. The pause after the user speaks decides whether the call feels like a conversation.

    Pick-up rate. If nobody answers, the best script does not matter. The caller number and the time of day are product decisions.

    Conversation score. Each call is scored against the eval set, with consent and with transcripts stored only for a limited time.

    The conversation itself has one narrow goal: find out what got in the way, offer a new slot, confirm it. Anything outside that goal is out of scope for the agent.

    05 · EvalsDefining good before building

    Before the first test I write an eval set of about 30 situations with the expected behaviour: excuses, anger, time pressure, follow-up questions and opt-outs. Every voice variant and every new version runs against it, and I record what changed per version. The cases below are drafts of the expected behaviour.

    06 · GuardrailsWhat the model is not allowed to decide

    The decision whether to call is deterministic and lives in a rules layer. The language model has no influence on it. That keeps the risky part small and testable.

    • Consent first. No call without explicit consent at setup. Data is deleted on request. Legal requirements for calls and data protection get checked before the test starts.
    • Limits. Quiet hours, a weekly call limit and an opt-out with one tap. The user also sets the highest escalation stage Callout may reach.
    • Agents can ask, not override. An agent may request a Callout. Consent, quiet hours and the weekly limit are checked by Callout itself.
    • Narrow conversation. A fixed goal and a list of forbidden topics. The call ends when the user objects.
    • Protection metrics. Opt-out rate, "too intrusive" feedback and uninstalls count as hard signals next to the success metrics.

    07 · InterfaceCallout as an MCP server

    Callout exposes its function to agents through MCP. The tool set is still under design. The working version covers four actions: assign a Callout to a habit, list open Callouts, cancel one, and fetch the result of a call. The result goes back to the agent, which writes the new slot into the calendar.

    Open questions: how an agent signs in on behalf of a user, how an agent is matched to a user, and how to stop agents from sending too many or unwanted requests.

    08 · AI product managementWhat the work is here

    Decide what the model may decide. Here it decides nothing about whether to call. The rest follows from this one choice.

    Define quality before you build. The eval set comes before the product and measures every change.

    Treat cost and latency as product constraints. Cost per call and time to first response choose the voice stack, not taste.

    Make trust a feature. Consent, limits and opt-out are part of the product, not legal fine print.

    Learn per user, simply. Counts per stage and time slot come first. Anything fancier has to beat them.

    Case study · Accenture hackathon · Agentic AI · Sales

    I built AME, an Account Memory Engine for Accenture client account leads

    In under 24 hours my team turned agentic AI for client account leads into a tangible solution, and it was awarded Best Idea. AME turns scattered meeting records into one account memory that keeps itself current.

    3 min readEvent: Accenture #ReinventionHackathonTime box: under 24 hours
    Question
    How does a client account lead keep the full picture of a client when the knowledge sits in transcripts, emails and notes?
    Approach
    A cross-functional team built a multi-agent system. It reads documents, turns them into knowledge items of an "Account Brain" and lets agents keep the central documents up to date.
    Result
    AME, built in under 24 hours and awarded Best Idea. It was one of five multi-agent solutions that came out of the hackathon.
    Lesson
    Let the agents keep the source-of-truth documents current. People refine the result instead of maintaining it.
    EventAccenture #ReinventionHackathon on agentic client account leadership and sales
    GoalUnderstand client needs better and reinvent our own processes and collaboration with AI
    TeamClient account leads, sales, GenAI architects and developers
    OutcomeFive multi-agent solutions in total. AME won Best Idea.

    01 · Starting pointWhat an account lead knows lives in many places

    Client account leads (CALs) steer the relationship with a client. What they need to know is spread over meeting transcripts, emails, notes and reports. The documents that should hold the big picture, such as org charts and power maps, only help if someone keeps them current after every conversation.

    Accenture hosted the #ReinventionHackathon to advance agentic client account leadership and sales. The aim was twofold: understand client needs more deeply, and reinvent our own processes and our collaboration with AI. Teams mixed account leads, sales, GenAI architects and developers, so the people who feel the problem sat next to the people who build the answer. Together the teams built five multi-agent solutions to raise productivity and quality.

    02 · The ideaOne account brain that updates itself

    AME takes the raw material of an account, such as transcripts, notes, emails and reports, and turns it into knowledge items in an "Account Brain". Agents then update the central source-of-truth documents on their own. Those documents become living, interactive artefacts, and the agents pick up follow-up tasks proactively.

    03 · ImpactWhat the account lead gets

    Smart guidance. Next best actions for account leadership, so the next conversation with the client has a clear purpose.

    Collective intelligence. Knowledge of the whole team in one place, not in the heads and inboxes of individuals.

    Precision. Automatic aggregation first, then manual refinement by the people who know the client. The machine does the collecting, the account lead does the judging.

    04 · TakeawaysWhat I take from it

    Automate the upkeep, keep the judgment. A document that has to be maintained by hand falls behind. A document that agents keep current and people refine stays useful.

    Put users and builders in one team. Account leads, sales and architects in the same room turned an idea into something tangible in under 24 hours.

    Start from the actions. The value of an account memory lies in the next best action it enables, not in the archive itself.

    Philipp Tschakert · Accenture #ReinventionHackathon. The team wants to drive these ideas further.

    Case study · Bachelor's thesis · TU Ilmenau, 2021

    I trained deep multi-layer perceptrons on Tesla T4 GPUs to improve bankruptcy forecasting

    I trained nine deep learning architectures to flag next-year bankruptcy from the balance-sheet ratios of listed US companies, and tested them against three methods banks already use.

    Philipp Tschakert7 min readPython · TensorFlow · scikit-learn
    Question
    Does deep learning predict bankruptcy better than the simpler models a bank runs today?
    Approach
    Partial replication of Barboza, Kimura and Altman (2017): 11 ratios, one-year horizon, a 3×3 grid of network depth and width.
    Result
    The best networks classify about 87% of test cases correctly. A random forest reaches 89% and trains in minutes, not hours.
    Lesson
    Model choice mattered least. Data preparation, honest evaluation and the cost of each error type decide whether a model is useful.
    76,942company-years from COMPUSTAT, 1985 to 2019
    4,771companies in the final dataset
    9 + 3network architectures plus benchmark models
    87.3%test accuracy of the best network (AUC 0.91)

    01Why the problem matters

    A bankruptcy hits lenders, suppliers, employees and shareholders at once. Banks price this risk into every loan, and auditors weigh it in every sign-off. Since Altman's Z-score in 1968, that has mostly meant a handful of ratios and a linear model.

    Neural networks can capture non-linear relationships between ratios that a linear model misses. They are also harder to tune and harder to explain. The thesis asks whether the gain in accuracy is worth that cost.

    02From raw data to training set

    The data are annual reports from COMPUSTAT, accessed through Wharton Research Data Services. A company counts as bankrupt if it was deleted from the database after a Chapter 7 or Chapter 11 filing. The model sees the ratios from the second-to-last available year and predicts the status one year later.

    The biggest obstacle is class imbalance. Bankruptcies are rare, and a model that always answers "solvent" would be 92% accurate and useless.

    03Eleven ratios as input

    The features follow Barboza et al.: the five Altman ratios for liquidity, profitability, productivity, leverage and asset turnover, plus six growth and change measures. A correlation check was meant to drop redundant variables. The highest correlation was 0.55, so everything stayed.

    That is a filter: it looks at the inputs only and ignores the model. I used two. The correlation check asks whether two ratios say the same thing. A Welch test per ratio asks whether solvent and bankrupt firms differ on average at all.

    How to select factors

    Three families of methods cover most cases. The thesis used the first, because the feature set came from the paper I was replicating. A neural network can also choose for itself: with 11 ratios, 20% dropout and weight updates over 100,000 epochs, a weak ratio mostly gets a small weight.

    04The experiment

    I built a multilayer perceptron in nine variants: two, four or eight hidden layers with 11, 33 or 110 neurons each. All use SELU activation, 20% dropout after the first layer, the Adam optimiser and binary cross-entropy loss. Training ran on a Tesla T4 GPU in Google Colab, 100,000 epochs per model with the whole training set as one batch.

    Benchmarks are a logistic regression, a random forest with 500 trees and XGBoost. All models are scored on the balanced test set by accuracy at threshold 0.5 and by area under the ROC curve.

    05Results

    More capacity helps, with one exception: the shallow, wide network M2_110 falls to nearly chance level.

    The large networks nearly memorise the training data. M8_110 reaches 99.1% there and 86.4% on the test set. That gap is the price of missing regularisation.

    Against the benchmarks, the random forest is slightly ahead on test accuracy.

    My reading: equal accuracy, and a clear edge for the random forest on effort and explainability. For tabular balance-sheet data of this size, a deep network has to show a measurable lead before it earns its complexity.

    06What a single number hides

    Accuracy treats every error alike. A bank does not. In the literature, a missed default costs about 35 times a rejected good customer. The random forest shows why this matters: it never raises a false alarm, but it misses more than one in five bankruptcies.

    07What I do differently now

    1. Baseline first, complexity second. A random forest with default settings was enough to keep up with eight layers of deep learning. "Do we need the complex model?" is now my first question.
    2. The metric is a product decision. Whether 87% is good depends on what an error costs and who pays for it. Settle that with the business side before training.
    3. Evaluation deserves as much care as the model. On small test sets, single cases decide the ranking. I now plan cross-validation, one shared evaluation for all models and tests for the data pipeline from day one.

    Case study · Master's thesis · Business informatics · TU Ilmenau

    I built a digital twin that keeps product data flowing after the sale

    I designed a digital twin for the indoor garden of our start-up GravityGarden. After the sale it keeps reporting how the device runs, carries services and can serve as a lab for researchers.

    8 min readMethod: design science researchRole: developer and technical co-founder
    Question
    How do you design a smart product so that it stays useful to the maker once it is in the customer's home?
    Approach
    Design science research in four phases. I built the system as I studied it: sensors, cloud backend, app prototype, and a link to a university remote lab.
    Result
    A working twin. Phases 1 to 3 (live monitoring, climate history, plant recognition) run on real and simulated data. Phase 4, adaptive lighting, is a concept.
    Lesson
    Start from the use case, then pick the sensor. Build a simulator early. Treat changed requirements as decisions, not errors.
    TopicDesign of a smart product-service system driven by a digital twin
    CaseGravityGarden, a start-up from the TU Ilmenau incubator
    ArtefactTwin with cloud backend, app prototype and a link to the GOLDi remote lab
    StackMQTT, Node-RED, Raspberry Pi, Firebase, FlutterFlow, Python, TensorFlow

    01 · Starting pointAfter the sale, the lights go out

    GravityGarden wants to bring controlled-environment agriculture into the living room: a device that controls light, climate and watering for plants. It is a biological and technical system, and whether it works only shows after weeks of use in the customer's home.

    That is where classic products lose the information flow. Development and manufacturing are well documented in CAD, PDM and bills of materials. Once the device ships, the maker knows almost nothing about it. Decisions on maintenance, improvements and the next generation rest on thin data.

    A digital twin is meant to close that gap. One distinction matters: not every digital copy is a twin. What counts is how data flows between device and copy.

    The second building block is the smart product-service system: a connected product sold together with digital services as one offer. The literature agrees the two belong together. Without usage data you are limited to basic services such as repair. With the twin, services like predictive maintenance or usage-based pricing become possible.

    02 · MethodResearch by building

    The thesis follows design science research in Österle's variant. I do not stand beside the object of study. I develop it. Knowledge comes from building an artefact, testing it against requirements, and then generalising.

    The theory base is a systematic literature review across six databases (ACM, AISeL, EBSCO, IEEE, Springer Link, Web of Science). Six core sources are the entry point, and further sources follow from them.

    03 · Business modelFrom data collector to solution provider

    Before choosing technology, you have to know what the data is for. Kampker et al. distinguish six business-model types for smart services, from selling raw data to orchestrating an entire ecosystem.

    For GravityGarden I chose the solution provider. The customer then does not buy a lamp with a water tank. They buy the outcome: plants that grow well. That requires the maker to analyse the data and act on it.

    04 · ArchitectureFrom sensor to app

    The concept became an architecture with a clear division of labour. Microcontrollers measure and switch. A Raspberry Pi acts as device controller and holds everything that must happen locally. The cloud stores the data of many devices, so each device sold can have its own twin instance.

    05 · ImplementationFour steps, each building on the last

    Digital twins fall into three classes by ambition: they inform, they support decisions, or they act on their own. The implementation climbs that ladder. Each phase delivers the data the next one needs.

    Two decisions sped up the work. First, I built simulated sensor values into every phase: temperature, humidity, fill levels and random plant scans. That let me develop the twin and demo it to stakeholders without setting up the hardware each time.

    Second, the design separates early between what users see and what the twin stores. The app shows a current value. Behind it sits the time series that later models learn from.

    Plant recognition took the most effort. The device has eight trays with three positions each. The tool head carries the camera and visits the positions while the chamber rotates.

    Phase 4 is worked out as a concept. A neural network should learn from historical data which light spectrum fits which plant and climate. In the simplest case, feedback is the weighed biomass. Later, taste, look and smell can join, rated by the user in the app. At that point the device becomes a learning system, and the user becomes part of product development.

    06 · Remote labThe garden as a lab for students

    One research question went beyond the product: does this approach open new ways to involve universities and researchers? To test it, I connected the garden to GOLDi, TU Ilmenau's Grid of Online Lab Devices, where students operate real devices over the internet.

    In the remote lab, the garden becomes an experiment setup. Students upload a configuration, an interpreter on the Raspberry Pi runs it, and they watch how the plants react.

    07 · LessonsWhat I do differently now

    Start with the use case. Sensors follow what the twin must be able to do later. If you fit sensors first and look for a use afterwards, you measure the wrong things.

    Simulation is a development tool. Simulated readings decoupled development and review from the hardware. Every demo got easier.

    Requirements move. The MQTT topics grew during implementation and differ from the original catalogue. The evaluation records such deviations as justified changes, not as errors.

    Plan for scale early. The prototype keeps all data in fast storage. With many devices, history belongs in cheap archive storage, or running costs grow with every reading.

    Name dependencies openly. If you build on a platform that is still being built, you inherit its limits. The decision was still right, because I made it together with the operators.

    The double role shaped me most. I was a researcher and the developer who had to make the system run. That mix of method, business model and working technology is exactly the work I look for in product management today.

    Case study · Project report · Connected car · Telecommunications

    I designed the tax API for a multi-tenant car connectivity platform

    Wi-Fi in a car sounds like a small feature. Behind it sits a platform that has to connect several carmakers, dozens of markets, three tax systems and a long technical chain from eSIM to hotspot.

    Philipp Tschakert2019 to 20218 min read
    Question
    What makes a connectivity platform for carmakers hard, once the technology works?
    Approach
    Two years at the interface of business and engineering: UX tests of the customer portals, solution design for new features, reporting in Power BI, process maps.
    Result
    Four problem areas decide quality: multi-tenancy, cross-border tax, the order-to-activation chain, and separating whose data traffic is billed to whom.
    Lesson
    Configuration beats custom builds. Regulation belongs in the product design. Quality is decided at the seams between systems.

    From 2019 to 2021 I worked at mm1 (valantic) in Connected Business, first as a working student, then as a junior consultant. My project was a connectivity platform of a large European telecom group. Through it, premium carmakers offer their customers mobile internet in the vehicle: the driver buys a data pass, and the car becomes a Wi-Fi hotspot for everyone on board.

    My work sat between business and engineering: UX tests of the customer portals with results presented to the client, solution design for new features, reporting in Power BI and process visualisations. I deliberately leave out client and product names. The problems are general enough to tell without them, and I still meet similar ones today.

    All graphics are simplified, anonymised illustrations of patterns typical for such platforms. They are not the original architecture.

    Problem 01One platform, many carmakers: multi-tenancy

    From the driver's side there is only their carmaker: its logo, language and tariffs. From the operator's side there is one platform with several carmakers as tenants. Each tenant brings several brands, each brand several markets, and each market its own languages, prices and legal texts.

    The core architecture question is: what is the same for everyone, and what may differ per tenant? The more sits in the shared core, the cheaper operations and development get. The more an individual tenant gets, the more the product feels like their own. The clean answer is configuration instead of custom development: a new carmaker or market should be one entry in the tenant model, not a separate code branch.

    Three things make multi-tenancy hard in practice. First, data separation: one carmaker's customer data must not be visible to any other, in the portal or in reporting. Second, release coupling: a feature one tenant urgently wants lands in a core everyone shares, so it has to be switchable. Third, combinatorics: testing a customer portal across several brands and markets never tests one portal, but a matrix. A bug often shows up in a single combination, say one brand in one language with one tariff.

    Problem 02Tax when customers and cars cross borders

    A data pass is a telecommunications service, and nearly every market taxes it differently. For a platform selling in Europe and North America, that means three fundamentally different systems.

    The EU has a shared VAT system, but each member state sets its own rate. Telecom services to private customers are taxed where the customer lives, not where the provider is based. The US has no national VAT. Instead, state, county and city sales taxes stack up, plus telecom-specific levies. Canada has a federal tax of 5 percent, and each province puts its own model on top.

    The car makes this more interesting than a phone contract, because it moves. A vehicle is registered in Germany and drives to Italy on holiday. A customer moves from Texas to California. A used car goes from Ontario to Québec and gets a new owner. In all these cases the network the car uses changes, but the place that counts for tax does not change automatically. In all three systems it hangs on the customer and their address, not on where the vehicle is.

    For product design this means more than it first seems. The platform needs a reliable tax location per customer: a verified address and rules for moves and changes of owner. Tax calculation belongs in its own module, configurable per market, not in the tariff logic. Even the interface is affected: in Europe the portal shows a final price, in North America a net price to which tax is added once the address is entered. The same order flow therefore looks different per market.

    Technology 01From the eSIM at the factory to Wi-Fi on the back seat

    For a driver to buy anything, the car must be prepared long before the sale. The SIM is an eSIM soldered into the control unit. Which mobile profile is active on it is controlled remotely under the GSMA standard. A vehicle can leave the factory with a starter profile and get the right local profile only in its target market.

    The chain is fragile because it crosses company boundaries: carmaker, mobile network, platform and payment provider are separate worlds. Every step needs a confirmation and a defined way to handle failure. In solution design for new features, that was usually the real work. The normal case takes minutes to describe. The questions after it take the time: what happens when payment succeeds but activation hangs? What does the customer see meanwhile, and who restarts the process?

    Technology 02BSS and OSS: who sells, who switches on, who counts

    Telecom platforms classically separate two layers. The business support system (BSS) knows customers, products and money: catalogue, orders, invoices, taxes, payments. The operations support system (OSS) knows the network: it activates services, collects usage data and monitors operations. Between them sits charging, which counts in real time how much data a vehicle uses.

    Charging is the part customers feel directly. When the allowance is used up or the pass has expired, the network must react within seconds: throttle or block the hotspot and send the user to a page where they can top up. At the same time the emergency call must never be affected, and a software update must not eat into the customer's paid volume. Separating the types of traffic is not an accounting detail. It is a precondition for a fair product.

    Technology 03Software updates: load balancing for a whole fleet

    Modern vehicles receive their software over the mobile network. One update can be several gigabytes. If a carmaker releases it to a whole fleet at once, load peaks in two places: in the radio cells where the cars stand, and on the servers that deliver the files.

    This is where all the earlier topics meet. The update is traffic on the carmaker's bill, so charging must separate it from the customer's hotspot traffic. The campaign runs per tenant, brand and market, so it depends on the tenant model. And network load is an operations topic in the OSS. Anyone who knows only one of these views designs a solution that fails on the other two.

    LessonsWhat I do differently now

    • The feature is rarely the problem. Wi-Fi in a car is technically solved. The hard part is tenants, markets, tax and billing around it.
    • Configuration beats special builds. Every exception for a tenant or market costs again with each further release. A good tenant model is the most important product decision of a platform.
    • Regulation belongs in the product design. Tax location and price display shape the order flow. If you consider them at the end, you build the flow twice.
    • Quality is made at the seams. Between order and activation, between carmaker and network, between the commercial and technical layers. Design the failure cases first there.

    This way of thinking has stayed with me: today I work in product management in financial services, again on a platform that serves many tenants and is heavily regulated. The industry is different. The questions are surprisingly similar.
